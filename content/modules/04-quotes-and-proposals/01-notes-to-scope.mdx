## The quote starts before the numbers

Bad quotes usually are not priced wrong. They are scoped wrong: something was left out that the customer assumed was in, or something was included that they never wanted. The fix is to write the scope first, in plain language, and get it right before a single number appears. This lesson does the scope. The next one does the price.

## What you have to work with

After a call or a site walkthrough you have notes. Fragments, abbreviations, maybe a voice memo you dictated in the truck. That is enough. The model is very good at turning fragments into structure, as long as you tell it what structure you want and forbid it from inventing what you did not say.

## The recipe

<Prompt title="Notes to scope of work">
Here is my business brief: [paste]

Below are my raw notes from a call or site visit with a prospective customer. Turn them into a scope of work with these sections: Summary (two sentences), What's included (bulleted, specific), What's not included (bulleted; infer only the obvious exclusions a customer commonly assumes are included in our kind of work, and mark each with "confirm"), Assumptions (access, timing, permits, anything the price depends on), and Open questions (things I still need to ask before I can price it).

Rules: use only what is in my notes and my brief. Do not add services I didn't mention. Do not state prices, dates, or durations. Where my notes are ambiguous, put the item under Open questions rather than guessing. Plain language a customer could read.

My notes:
[paste or dictate]
</Prompt>

<Callout kind="note">
The "What's not included" section, with its "confirm" flags, is where most of the value lives. It surfaces the assumptions you would otherwise discover on the job, when they cost money.
</Callout>

## Read it like the customer will

The thirty-second check applies, with one addition: read the scope as if you were the customer and ask what you would expect to be included that is not listed. If you find one, either add it or move it to "not included" with a clear note. Ambiguity in a scope is not neutral; it resolves in the customer's favor once the job is underway.

## Dictating your notes

If you take notes by voice, dictate straight into the tool or into your phone's notes app. Do not tidy them up. "Two-story, north side needs new flashing, they want it done before the party on the 20th, asked about gutters but budget-conscious, dog in yard" is exactly the input this recipe wants. Cleaning it up first is wasted work.

<Worksheet title="Scope a real job">
- Take the notes from the last quote you sent (or the next call you take).
- Run the scope recipe. Read the "not included" and "open questions" sections carefully.
- Compare against the quote you actually sent. What did the recipe catch that you missed, or the reverse?
- Save the recipe. Add a CHECK: "Every 'confirm' item resolved before pricing."
</Worksheet>
