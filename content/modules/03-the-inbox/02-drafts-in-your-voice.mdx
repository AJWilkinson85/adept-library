## Five replies cover most of the inbox

Count for a week and you will find that a handful of situations account for most of the typing: a new inquiry, a scheduling change, a price question, a complaint, and a "just checking in." Each gets a recipe. Once the five exist, the office stops writing from scratch.

## The rule for all of them

The draft comes from the model. The decision comes from you. The recipe never decides whether to offer a refund, move a date, or waive a fee; it asks you, or it takes the decision as an input. Watch for drafts that quietly make a decision for you ("We'd be happy to waive that") and cut them.

## Recipe: the new inquiry

<Prompt title="Reply to a new inquiry">
Here is my business brief: [paste]

A prospective customer wrote the message below. Draft a reply in my voice that: thanks them briefly (no gushing), answers anything I can answer from the brief, asks the two or three questions I would need before quoting, and proposes a specific next step (a call, a site visit, or a quote by a date). Under 140 words. Do not quote a price unless it is on the price list. Do not promise a date. Do not use the phrase "reach out."

Their message:
[paste]

After the draft, list anything I need to confirm or fill in before sending.
</Prompt>

## Recipe: the complaint

Complaints are where tone matters most and where generic drafts do the most damage.

<Prompt title="Reply to a complaint">
Here is my business brief: [paste]

A customer sent the complaint below. Draft a reply in my voice that: acknowledges the specific problem in their words (not "we're sorry for any inconvenience"), takes responsibility for what is ours without admitting to what is not, states exactly what happens next and when, and gives them a direct way to reach me. Under 150 words. No excuses about being busy. Do not offer money, credit, or free work; I decide that separately and will add it.

Their message:
[paste]

What I've decided to do about it: [your decision, or "nothing yet, just acknowledge"]
</Prompt>

<Callout kind="note">
Notice the decision line at the bottom. That is you, making the call. The tool writes it up. That division is the difference between using AI well and letting it run your customer relationships.
</Callout>

## Recipe: the price question

Same shape: brief, their message, constraints. The key constraint: "Only quote prices from the attached price list. If the request needs a custom quote, say so and ask for [what you need]." Module 4 goes deep on actual quoting.

## Recipe: the scheduling change and the check-in

These two are short enough that after a few runs your people will stop needing the tool for them, which is fine. The recipes still belong in the library so a new hire has them on day one.

<Worksheet title="Build the five">
- Pull one real example of each of the five situations from the last month.
- Run the inquiry and complaint recipes on the real messages. Apply the thirty-second check.
- Write the price-question, scheduling, and check-in recipes yourself using the same shape. Keep each PROMPT under 150 words.
- Add all five to the prompt library. Have someone else run the inquiry recipe cold.
</Worksheet>
