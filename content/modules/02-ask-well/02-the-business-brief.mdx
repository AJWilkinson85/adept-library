## The problem with starting from zero

The model forgets you between conversations. Retyping context every time is the reason most people give up after a week. The fix is a single document, written once and refined over a month, that you paste at the top of any real work. I call it the business brief. Every serious user of these tools has one, whether they call it that or not.

## What goes in it

Keep it to one page. Plain text. No formatting that will get lost when pasted.

**Who we are.** Business name, what you sell, where, to whom, how long you have been at it, how many people. Two sentences.

**How we sound.** Three or four adjectives, then two short real examples of your writing. "Direct, warm, no corporate language. We say 'you' a lot. We never say 'reach out.'"

**How we work.** The rules a new hire learns in week one. Payment terms. What you do not do. Minimums. Service area. Lead times. Warranty. The things customers ask about most.

**Prices and offers.** Your standard prices or price ranges, packages, and any current offer. If the list is long, attach the price sheet instead and say "see attached price list."

**People.** First names and roles of anyone who might be mentioned. "Dana handles scheduling. Marcus is lead tech."

**Never.** Things the tool must never do or say on your behalf: quote a price not on the list, promise a date, mention a competitor by name, offer a discount, use exclamation points.

<Callout kind="warn">
Do not put customer names, account numbers, or anything from someone else's private information in the brief. It is about you, and it will be pasted a lot. Module 6 covers the line in detail.
</Callout>

## Getting a first draft in five minutes

You do not have to write it from scratch.

<Prompt title="Draft my business brief">
Interview me to build a one-page "business brief" I can paste into future conversations so you understand my business. Ask me one question at a time, no more than twelve questions total, covering: what we do and for whom, how we sound (I'll paste examples), our working rules (terms, minimums, service area, lead times), prices or packages, key people and roles, and things you must never do on our behalf. When you have enough, write the brief in plain text under 400 words with those six headings.
</Prompt>

Answer the questions honestly and briefly. Then edit the result by hand. The first version is 80 percent right; the next month of using it gets it to 95.

## How to use it

Start every work conversation with "Here is my business brief:" and paste it, then attach any documents, then ask for what you need. If your tool has a projects or custom-instructions feature, put the brief there once and it will apply automatically. Either way, the brief is the thing that makes every later module work.

<Worksheet title="Build the brief">
- Run the interview prompt and produce a first draft.
- Cut anything that is not true or not stable. If it changes weekly, it does not belong.
- Add two real writing samples under "How we sound."
- Save it as a plain text file named business-brief.txt where your whole office can find it.
- Use it in the next three conversations you have with the tool. Fix whatever it gets wrong about you.
</Worksheet>
