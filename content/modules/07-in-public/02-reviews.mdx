## The math

A business with 40 reviews and a 4.7 gets the call over a business with 9 reviews and a 5.0. Most owners know this and still do not ask, because asking feels awkward and replying feels like a chore. Both are recipe work.

## Asking

The ask goes out the day after a job you are proud of, from you, by text or email, short. The tool's job is to write it once in your voice with three variations so it does not read as automated when the same customer refers a neighbor.

<Prompt title="Review request, three versions">
Here is my business brief: [paste]

Write three short versions of a message asking a customer for a review after a completed job. Each under 60 words, in my voice, one sentence of thanks that references "the job" generically, one sentence asking for a review with the link [LINK], and one sentence that makes it easy to say no. No pressure, no "it would mean the world." Versions: one for text message, one for email, one for a customer who has already used us more than once.
</Prompt>

Save all three in the prompt library, fill in the link once, and send by hand. Do not automate the sending. The customer can tell.

## Answering

Every review gets a reply within a week. Good ones get two sentences. Bad ones get the most careful paragraph you write all month, and this is where the tool earns its keep, because your first draft of a reply to an unfair review is not one you should publish.

<Prompt title="Reply to a review">
Here is my business brief: [paste]

Here is a review we received, with the star rating: [paste]

Here is what actually happened, from our side, told plainly: [paste, including anything we got wrong]

Draft a public reply. If the review is positive: two sentences, specific to what they mentioned, no "we strive." If it is negative: acknowledge the specific thing without arguing, say what we did or will do about it, and offer to continue by phone with a name and number. No defending, no explaining their mistake, no "we're sorry you feel that way." Under 100 words. Then tell me in one line whether anything in my account of events should stay private.
</Prompt>

<Callout kind="warn">
Never paste a customer's full name, address, or invoice details into the tool alongside a dispute. The review is already public; your internal notes are not. Module 6's line applies, and disputes are where people forget it.
</Callout>

## The rule

Two tiers, like everything else. Positive review replies are Tier 1: the tool drafts, someone skims and posts. Negative review replies are Tier 2: the owner reads every word, sleeps on it if the rating is one star, and posts the next morning.

<Worksheet title="Set up the review routine">
- Generate your three ask messages and save them with the link filled in.
- Send the ask to the last three customers you were proud of the work for.
- Find your oldest unanswered review and reply to it with the recipe.
- If you have a negative review with no reply, draft one, sleep on it, post it.
- Add both recipes to the library with tiers marked.
</Worksheet>
