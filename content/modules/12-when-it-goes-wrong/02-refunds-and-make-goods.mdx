## The expensive way to decide

When a job goes wrong and the customer is upset, the owner on the phone offers whatever ends the call: a full refund for a partial problem, a free return visit that costs more than the job. Or the opposite: digs in over $80 and loses a $4,000-a-year customer. Both come from deciding in the moment. The fix is a short written policy, and the tool helps you write one that you will actually use.

## The policy

Three or four tiers, plain language, based on what went wrong rather than how loud the customer is. "We got it wrong and it is fixable: we fix it free, first available slot. We got it wrong and it is not fixable: refund the affected part of the job. It was not our fault but the customer is unhappy: [what you are willing to do, if anything]. The customer is asking for something outside these: the owner decides, within a day."

<Prompt title="Draft our make-good policy">
Here is my business brief: [paste]

Here are the last five or six times a job went wrong and what we did about it, roughly: [paste]

Help me write a make-good policy for my team. Group what happened into three or four kinds of situation based on whose fault it was and whether it can be fixed. For each, propose one standard response we can offer without asking the owner, and a limit (dollars or hours) above which the owner decides. Point out where my past responses were inconsistent with each other. Write the final policy as a paragraph under 200 words a new employee could apply on the phone.
</Prompt>

The inconsistency list is uncomfortable and useful. It usually shows that the customer who complained loudest got the most, which is the policy you are replacing.

<Callout kind="note">
Consumer protection rules on refunds vary by state and by what you sell. The policy is your floor for how you treat people, not a substitute for what the law requires. If you sell goods rather than services, check the rules for your state once.
</Callout>

## The message

Once the policy exists, the message to the customer is short: what we are doing, when, and who to call. The complaint reply from the previous lesson already ends with "what we will do"; the policy is where that line comes from. When the policy covers it, the office can send it. When it does not, it goes to the owner with the policy attached and a one-line recommendation.

## Tracking

One line per make-good in a simple log: date, job, what went wrong, what we did, cost. Quarterly, paste the log and ask what pattern it shows. Three refunds for the same kind of mistake is not a customer problem; it is a procedure problem, and Module 8 is where you fix it.

<Worksheet title="Write the policy">
- List the last five times a job went wrong and what you did.
- Run the prompt. Read the inconsistencies. Edit the policy until you would defend every line.
- Put the policy in the prompt library next to the complaint recipe.
- Start the make-good log. One line per event.
- Tell the team the policy in person, in five minutes.
</Worksheet>
