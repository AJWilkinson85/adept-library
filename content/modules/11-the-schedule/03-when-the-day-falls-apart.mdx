## The morning nobody plans for

The week was drafted, the messages went out, and at 8:40 one person is out and one job just doubled. Now every downstream job on that person's list is at risk and you have to decide, fast, what moves, what waits, and who gets told. The tool does not know the answer. It knows how to lay out the options faster than you can while also answering the phone.

## Re-plan, do not re-draft

Paste today's schedule, say what changed, and ask for options rather than a new plan.

<Prompt title="Re-plan the day">
Here is my business brief, including how we schedule: [paste]

Here is today's schedule as planned: [paste]

What changed at [time]: [describe, plainly: who is out, which job is running long and by how much, what is missing].

Give me two or three ways to handle today, each in four lines: what moves, what gets pushed to another day, who does what, and which customers need a message. Order them from least disruption to customers to most. For each option, name the one thing that could go wrong with it. Then draft the customer messages for the option you would pick, using our "running late" and "reschedule" templates: [paste them].
</Prompt>

Pick one. Send the messages. Go. Total elapsed time, including reading: three or four minutes.

<Callout kind="note">
The "one thing that could go wrong" line is the reason to ask for options rather than a plan. Option 1 is usually the cleanest and also the one that assumes the running-long job finishes by noon. If you know it will not, you take option 2 without having to think it through from scratch.
</Callout>

## Afterward

At the Friday review (Module 9), the re-plans show up as a pattern. If the same tech is out two Fridays in three, or the same kind of job always runs long, that is a scheduling rule waiting to be written into the brief: "Book [job type] with a two-hour buffer." The re-plan prompt handles the emergency; the brief slowly makes them rarer.

## Keeping the crew informed

The same three-line summary of what changed goes to the people whose day moved, by text, before they find out from a customer. The tool can format it from the option you picked. Nobody on the crew cares about the options; they care about where they are going next and by when.

<Worksheet title="Practice on last week">
- Take a day last week that fell apart. Reconstruct the morning schedule and what changed.
- Run the re-plan prompt as if it were 9am. Compare what it suggests to what you actually did.
- Save the recipe with today's four scheduling messages pasted into it, so it is ready when you need it and not while the phone is ringing.
- At the next Friday review, look for the pattern behind the disruption and write the rule.
</Worksheet>
