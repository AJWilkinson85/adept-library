## Where the tool is actually useful for thinking

The model is a poor oracle and a good sparring partner. Ask it "should I buy the second truck" and you get a balanced non-answer. Ask it to make the strongest case against buying the truck, using your numbers, and it will say the thing your accountant was too polite to say. Use it for the argument, not the verdict.

## The one-page memo

For any decision over a threshold you set (a month of profit is a reasonable one), write a one-page memo before you decide. Not for anyone else; for you, so that in a year you can read why you did it. The tool drafts it from your thinking out loud, then argues with it.

<Prompt title="Decision memo, both sides">
Here is my business brief: [paste]

The decision: [state it in one sentence].
What I am leaning toward and why, unpolished: [paste].
The numbers I have: [paste: cost, expected return, timeline, what happens if I am wrong, anything else].

Write a one-page decision memo with four sections. THE CASE FOR, using only my numbers and reasoning, tightened. THE CASE AGAINST, the strongest version an experienced operator in my industry would make, including anything my numbers assume that might not hold. WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE for the "for" case to work out; list three or four conditions I could check before deciding. THE QUESTION I AM AVOIDING: one question, based on my own words, that I seem to be steering around. Do not recommend a decision.
</Prompt>

Read the "against" section twice. If it did not land a punch, you did not give it enough of your real numbers.

## The conditions are the work

"What would have to be true" turns a gut call into a short checklist. If buying the truck requires a second crew, and a second crew requires a foreman you do not have, then the decision is not about the truck. Most bad decisions are good decisions about the wrong question, and this section finds the right one.

<Callout kind="note">
Numbers in, better argument out. If the memo is vague, the input was vague. Pull the actual figures from the books routine in Module 5 before you run this, not estimates from memory.
</Callout>

## Deciding anyway

The memo does not decide. You do, the next morning, after sleeping on it. Then write the decision and the date at the top of the memo and file it. When the decision turns out well or badly a year later, read it. That is how judgment gets built, and no tool does that part for you.

<Worksheet title="Write one memo">
- Pick a decision you are currently circling. Anything over your threshold.
- Gather real numbers. Ten minutes in the books, not from memory.
- Run the prompt. Read the "against" and the avoided question honestly.
- Check the "what would have to be true" conditions. At least one will be checkable this week.
- Decide, date it, file it. Set a reminder to reread it in a year.
</Worksheet>
